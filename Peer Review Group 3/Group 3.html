1. Introduction¶

As fossil fuel reserves continue to decline, the direct production of dimethyl ether (DME) from carbon dioxide and hydrogen has attracted growing attention from both the scientific community and industry. DME functions not merely as a valuable chemical intermediate, but also stands out as an attractive option for blending with or replacing traditional liquid fuels like diesel. Its physicochemical characteristics and combustion behavior including a cetane rating of 55 and reduced emissions of nitrogen oxides, sulfur oxides, and carbon monoxide make it a compelling environmentally-friendly fuel alternative.[1–7]

That said, DME's extremely low boiling point (−24.8°C) represents a significant drawback when compared to synthetic fuels generated through Fischer–Tropsch processes, as this property demands considerable changes to current fuel distribution and storage systems.[1] Moreover, should atmospheric CO₂ capture prove economically viable at industrial levels, this production route could simultaneously help combat climate change while supplying a renewable carbon feedstock that does not rely on limited fossil resources. Consequently, worldwide DME output presently standing at roughly 5–7 million tons each year is projected to expand in the foreseeable future.[1]

1.1 Reaction Scheme¶

The following reaction scheme occurs on a bifunctional catalyst, with methanol (MeOH) acting as an intermediate compound:[3]

Direct CO₂ hydrogenation to methanol: $$\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{OH} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad \Delta\text{H}_{\text{R},298\text{K}} = -49.4\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1} \tag{1}$$ CO hydrogenation to methanol: $$\text{CO} + 2\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{OH} \quad \Delta\text{H}_{\text{R},298\text{K}} = -90.6\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1} \tag{2}$$ Reverse water-gas shift (RWGS): $$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{CO} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad \Delta\text{H}_{\text{R},298\text{K}} = 41.2\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1} \tag{3}$$ Methanol dehydration to DME: $$2\text{CH}_3\text{OH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{OCH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad \Delta\text{H}_{\text{R},298\text{K}} = -23.4\,\text{kJ}\,\text{mol}^{-1} \tag{4}$$

1.2 Thermodynamic Insights¶

Reaction (1) depicts the straightforward hydrogenation of CO₂ to methanol, releasing heat in an exothermic process. Thermodynamically, this favors elevated temperatures and reduced pressures to maximize CO₂ conversion. Alternatively, methanol can be generated through an indirect pathway involving the reverse water-gas shift (RWGS) reaction shown in Eq. (3), with subsequent hydrogenation of the CO produced following Eq. (2).

While the RWGS reaction absorbs heat (endothermic) without altering the mole count, methanol formation is highly exothermic and reduces the overall number of molecules. These contrasting thermodynamic characteristics create opposing temperature and pressure dependencies, limiting conversion according to equilibrium constraints.

The overall yield benefits from the dehydration of methanol to DME described in Eq. (4), which drives the equilibrium forward by continuously consuming methanol. This dehydration reaction is also exothermic.[1]

1.3 Industrial Operating Conditions¶

Industrial operation of this process generally employs:

  • Pressure range: 3–7 MPa
  • Temperature range: 240–280°C
  • Catalyst type: Copper-based catalysts dispersed on metal oxide supports (e.g.,ZnO and Al₂O₃)[3]

Incorporating a water-conducting membrane presents an attractive avenue for process enhancement; however, maintaining membrane integrity under the harsh conditions needed for DME production poses a significant technical hurdle.[3,4]

Research by Banivaheb et al.[2] showed that implementing a Na⁺-functionalized water-conducting membrane enables CO₂ conversion to surpass thermodynamic equilibrium limitations. Their investigation utilized a Cu/Zn/HZSM-5 catalyst with: the reactor was fed with a CO2/H2 mixture at a molar ratio of 1:3 and a gas hourly space velocity of 4200 L kg-1cat h-1. Under operating conditions of 3.5 MPa and 230 °C, the incorporation of the membrane increased CO2 conversion from 20.7% to 53.2%.

The present study models this process for a facility targeting an annual DME output of 1000 tonnes. Initially, the equilibrium DME yield is determined as a function of temperature, pressure, and feed composition, since equilibrium represents the theoretical maximum achievable under specified reaction conditions.

2. Method¶

To analyze the process, we simulate the equilibrium DME yield by varying three key parameters: temperature, pressure, and feed composition. The temperature is examined across a range of 200 to 300 °C, while the pressure is set between 10 and 70 bar. The feed composition itself is expressed through the molar ratio of CO₂ to H₂ entering the reactor. To capture this in a quantifiable manner, a ratio factor $F_r$ is introduced defined in Eq. (5) and allowed to vary from 0.33 up to 2.

$$\text{CO}_2 = F_r \text{H}_2 \tag{5}$$

Starting from a target annual DME production of 1000 tonnes, we estimate the total inlet molar flow rate, denoted $\dot{n}_{in,total}$. For our initial calculation, we assume a DME yield of 40% together with a ratio factor of 1. Under continuous operation, this translates to a required feed rate of roughly 6.0 mol s⁻¹ of CO₂ and H₂ entering the reactor.

Whenever the feed composition is varied, the total inlet molar flow rate is held constant. The individual flow rates of CO₂ and H₂, expressed as functions of the ratio factor, are then given by Eq. (6) and Eq. (7), respectively.

$$\dot{n}_{\text{CO}_2} = \frac{1}{1 + \frac{1}{F_r}} \, \dot{n}_{\text{in,total}} \tag{6}$$

$$\dot{n}_{\text{H}_2} = \frac{1}{1 + F_r} \, \dot{n}_{\text{in,total}} \tag{7}$$

2.1 Equilibrium and Composition Modeling¶

The equilibrium coefficient, $K_{x,j}$, is a reaction-dependent parameter governed by the system's temperature and pressure. Upon reaching equilibrium, the mixture achieves a specific composition, $\underline{x}$, which must satisfy the equilibrium requirements for every reaction $j$ taking place in the system. These conditions are defined as follows:

$$K_{x,1} = \frac{x_{\text{MeOH}} \, x_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}}{x_{\text{CO}_2} \, x_{\text{H}_2}^3} \tag{8}$$

$$K_{x,2} = \frac{x_{\text{MeOH}}}{x_{\text{CO}} \, x_{\text{H}_2}^2} \tag{9}$$

$$K_{x,3} = \frac{x_{\text{CO}} \, x_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}}{x_{\text{CO}_2} \, x_{\text{H}_2}} \tag{10}$$

$$K_{x,4} = \frac{x_{\text{DME}} \, x_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}}{x_{\text{MeOH}}^2} \tag{11}$$

This framework generates a system of four equations containing five unknown mole fractions; however, the composition of the sixth component can be expressed in terms of the others. By utilizing the reaction extent ($\xi$), the composition of all components can be simplified and expressed using only four independent variables. The general form of this relationship is shown in Equation (12), where $\nu_{i,j}$ signifies the stoichiometric coefficient of component $i$ within reaction $j$:

$$x_{i,\text{out}} = \frac{\dot{n}_{i,\text{out}}}{\dot{n}_{\text{out}}} = \frac{\dot{n}_{\text{in}} + \sum_j \xi_j \nu_{i,j}}{\dot{n}_{\text{in}} - \sum_j \xi_j \sum_i \nu_{i,j}} \tag{12}$$

2.2 Stoichiometric Matrix Analysis¶

By analyzing the rank of the stoichiometric coefficient matrix $\underline{N}$, the number of independent reactions and key components can be identified. This allows for a significant reduction in the complexity of the governing equations.

The stoichiometric coefficient matrix $\underline{N}$ is defined as:

$$ \underline{N} = \begin{array}{r} \text{CO}_2 \\ \text{CO} \\ \text{H}_2 \\ \text{H}_2\text{O} \\ \text{CH}_3\text{OH} \\ \text{CH}_3\text{OCH}_3 \end{array} \begin{array}{c} \begin{array}{cccc} R_1 & R_2 & R_3 & R_4 \end{array} \\ \left[ \begin{array}{rrrr} -1 & 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 1 & 0 \\ -3 & -2 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & -2 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right] \end{array} \tag{13} $$

3. Reaction Analysis¶

3.1 Thermodynamic properties¶

To evaluate the thermodynamics of the reaction system, temperature-dependent properties such as heat capacity ($C_p$), entropy ($S$), and enthalpy ($H$) were first computed using Glenn equations from the NASA database [9] and then compared with computed values from Shomate equations from the NIST database [8].

$$\frac{C_p^0(T)}{R} = a_1 T^{-2} + a_2 T^{-1} + a_3 + a_4 T^1 + a_5 T^2 + a_6 T^3 + a_7 T^4 \tag{14}$$

$$\frac{H^0(T)}{RT} = -a_1 T^{-2} + \frac{a_2 \ln(T)}{T} + a_3 + \frac{a_4 T^1}{2} + \frac{a_5 T^2}{3} + \frac{a_6 T^3}{4} + \frac{a_7 T^4}{5} + \frac{b_1}{T} \tag{15}$$

$$\frac{S^0(T)}{R} = \frac{-a_1 T^{-2}}{2} - a_2 T^{-1} + a_3 \ln(T) + a_4 T + \frac{a_5 T^2}{2} + \frac{a_6 T^3}{3} + \frac{a_7 T^4}{4} + b_2 \tag{16}$$

3.2 Thermodynamic Properties (Shomate Equations)¶

Thermodynamic properties are implemented in Python using the Shomate equations:

$$c_p = A + B\vartheta + C\vartheta^2 + D\vartheta^3 + E\vartheta^{-2} \tag{17}$$

$$H - H(298.15\,\text{K}) = A\vartheta + \frac{B\vartheta^2}{2} + \frac{C\vartheta^3}{3} + \frac{D\vartheta^4}{4} - E\vartheta^{-1} + F - H \tag{18}$$

$$S = A\ln(\vartheta) + B\vartheta + \frac{C\vartheta^2}{2} + \frac{D\vartheta^3}{3} - \frac{E\vartheta^{-2}}{2} + G \tag{19}$$

def rxn_data(T):
    res = np.empty([3,5]) # generate empty vector of required size
    # call the function on thermodynamic properties for all components
    data_CO2 = components['CO2'].calculate(T)
    data_CO = components['CO'].calculate(T)
    data_H2 = components['H2'].calculate(T)
    data_H2O = components['H2O'].calculate(T)
    data_CH3OH = components['CH3OH'].calculate(T)
    data_CH3OCH3 = components['DME'].calculate(T)

    # use stoichiometry to calculate the thermodynamic properties of reaction 1
    res[0,0] = data_CH3OH[0] + data_H2O[0] - data_CO2[0] - 3*data_H2[0] # reaction heat capacity in J/mol K
    res[0,1] = data_CH3OH[1] + data_H2O[1] - data_CO2[1] - 3*data_H2[1] # reaction enthalpy in J/mol
    res[0,2] = data_CH3OH[2] + data_H2O[2] - data_CO2[2] - 3*data_H2[2] # reaction entropy in J/mol K
    res[0,3] = res[0,1] - T*res[0,2] # reaction Gibbs enthalpy in J/mol
    res[0,4] = np.exp(-res[0,3]/(8.3145*T)) # reaction equilibrium constant

    # use stoichiometry to calculate the thermodynamic properties of reaction 3
    res[1,0] = data_CO[0] + data_H2O[0] - data_CO2[0] - data_H2[0] # reaction heat capacity in J/mol K
    res[1,1] = data_CO[1] + data_H2O[1] - data_CO2[1] - data_H2[1] # reaction enthalpy in J/mol
    res[1,2] = data_CO[2] + data_H2O[2] - data_CO2[2] - data_H2[2] # reaction entropy in J/mol K
    res[1,3] = res[1,1] - T*res[1,2] # reaction Gibbs enthalpy in J/mol
    res[1,4] = np.exp(-res[1,3]/(8.3145*T)) # reaction equilibrium constant

    # use stoichiometry to calculate the thermodynamic properties of reaction 4
    res[2,0] = data_CH3OCH3[0] + data_H2O[0] - 2*data_CH3OH[0]  # reaction heat capacity in J/mol K
    res[2,1] = data_CH3OCH3[1] + data_H2O[1] - 2*data_CH3OH[1]  # reaction enthalpy in J/mol
    res[2,2] = data_CH3OCH3[2] + data_H2O[2] - 2*data_CH3OH[2]  # reaction entropy in J/mol K
    res[2,3] = res[2,1] - T*res[2,2] # reaction Gibbs enthalpy in J/mol
    res[2,4] = np.exp(-res[2,3]/(8.3145*T)) # reaction equilibrium constant

    return res

def calculate_reaction_extents(xi, n_in, T, p, ratio):
    """
    Solves the non-linear system for reaction extents.
    xi: array of extents [xi1, xi2, xi3]
    """
    # 1. Get current composition and thermodynamic constants
    x = composition(xi, n_in) # Assumed defined elsewhere
    rxn_data_get = rxn_data(T)
    k_eq = rxn_data_get[:, -1]

    # 2. Map indices to readable names (example mapping based on your previous code)
    # x = [CO2, CO, CH3OH, H2, H2O, DME]
    comp = {
        'CO2': x[0], 'CO': x[1], 'CH3OH': x[2],
        'H2': x[3], 'H2O': x[4], 'DME': x[5]
    }
        # 3. Define the equilibrium expressions
    # Form: K_eq * (Reactants) - (Products) = 0
    # Rxn 1: CO2 + 3H2 <-> CH3OH + H2O
    r1 = k_eq[0] * (p**2) * (comp['CO2'] * comp['H2']**3) - (comp['CH3OH'] * comp['H2O'])

    # Rxn 3: CO2 + H2 <-> CO + H2O
    r3 = k_eq[1] * (p**0) * (comp['CO2'] * comp['H2']) - (comp['CO'] * comp['H2O'])

    # Rxn 4: 2CH3OH <-> DME + H2O
    r4 = k_eq[2] * (p**0) * (comp['CH3OH']**2) - (comp['DME'] * comp['H2O'])

    return np.array([r1, r3, r4])

3.3 Determination of Equillibrium yield and conversion

With the reaction equilibrium coefficient, all the information needed to solve the set of equations is provided. For each combination of temperature, pressure and inlet composition a solvent vector is obtained, which consists of the reaction extent of the three key reactions. These are used to determine the equilibrium composition, which can then be used to estimate the DME yield with respect to $CO_2$ $Y_{DME,CO_2}$ and the $CO_2$ conversion $U_{CO_2}$ with the help of Eq. (19) and (20). [6]

$$Y_{DME,CO_2} = \frac{\sum_j \nu_{CO_2,j}}{\sum_j \nu_{DME,j}} \frac{\dot{n}_{DME,in} - \dot{n}_{DME}}{\dot{n}_{CO_2,in}} \quad (20)$$

$$U_{CO_2} = \frac{\dot{n}_{CO_2,in} - \dot{n}_{CO_2}}{\dot{n}_{CO_2,in}} \quad (21)$$

import numpy as np
from itertools import product
from scipy.optimize import root

def run_simulation(pressures, temperatures, ratios, n_in_array):
    """
    Executes the equilibrium simulation over a grid of P, T, and Ratios.
    """
    # Initialize output structures
    shape = (len(pressures), len(temperatures), len(ratios))
    xi = np.zeros((*shape, 3))
    x_out = np.zeros((*shape, 6))

    xi_guess = np.array([0.35, 0.02, 0.15])

    # Using itertools.product to replace nested loops
    for i, (pp, TT, rr) in enumerate(product(range(len(pressures)),
                                            range(len(temperatures)),
                                            range(len(ratios)))):
        # Solver call
        args = (n_in_array[rr], temperatures[TT], pressures[pp], ratios[rr])
        res = root(rxn_ext, xi_guess, args=args, method='lm')

        # Check for successful convergence before assigning results
        if res.success:
            xi[pp, TT, rr] = res.x
            x_out[pp, TT, rr] = composition(res.x, n_in_array[rr])
        else:
            # Assign NaN to indicate non-convergence
            xi[pp, TT, rr] = np.nan
            x_out[pp, TT, rr] = np.nan * np.ones(6) # Assuming 6 components

    # Vectorized Post-Processing
    # n_in_sum_per_ratio: total inlet molar flow for each ratio (shape: (len(ratios),))
    n_in_sum_per_ratio = np.sum(n_in_array, axis=1)

    # Broadcast n_in_sum_per_ratio to (1, 1, len(ratios), 1) to align with xi and x_out
    n_in_sum_broadcast = n_in_sum_per_ratio[np.newaxis, np.newaxis, :, np.newaxis]

    # n_out calculation: x_out * (total_inlet_moles - 2 * extent_of_reaction_1)
    # The term (n_in_sum_broadcast - 2 * xi[..., 0:1]) broadcasts to (len(p), len(T), len(ratio), 1)
    # n_out will have shape (len(p), len(T), len(ratio), num_components)
    n_out = x_out * (n_in_sum_broadcast - 2 * xi[..., 0:1])

    # Y calculation (Conversion of Methanol, based on CO2 feed and MeOH outlet)
    # n_in_MeOH: Methanol inlet flow (n_in_array[:, 5], which is 0 for this problem setup)
    n_in_MeOH = n_in_array[:, 5] # Shape (len(ratios),)
    n_in_MeOH_broadcast = n_in_MeOH[np.newaxis, np.newaxis, :, np.newaxis] # Shape (1, 1, len(ratios), 1)
    n_out_MeOH_broadcast = n_out[..., 5][:, :, :, np.newaxis] # Shape (len(p), len(T), len(ratios), 1)

    # n_in_CO2: CO2 inlet flow (n_in_array[:, 0])
    n_in_CO2 = n_in_array[:, 0] # Shape (len(ratios),)
    n_in_CO2_broadcast = n_in_CO2[np.newaxis, np.newaxis, :, np.newaxis] # Shape (1, 1, len(ratios), 1)

    # Y will have shape (len(p), len(T), len(ratios), 1) after broadcasting
    Y = ((-2/1) * (n_in_MeOH_broadcast - n_out_MeOH_broadcast)) / n_in_CO2_broadcast

    # U calculation (Conversion of CO2)
    n_out_CO2_broadcast = n_out[..., 0][:, :, :, np.newaxis] # Shape (len(p), len(T), len(ratios), 1)

    # U will have shape (len(p), len(T), len(ratios), 1) after broadcasting
    U = (n_in_CO2_broadcast - n_out_CO2_broadcast) / n_in_CO2_broadcast

    return xi, x_out, n_out, Y, U

Analysis of Equilibrium Constants¶

For reaction (1), the analysis shows a significant increase in the chemical equilibrium constant as pressure rises, particularly at lower temperatures, which is consistent with Eq. (14).

In contrast, the equilibrium constants for the other two reactions are essentially independent of pressure, as the total number of moles is identical on both the reactant and product sides. The impact of temperature is summarized below:

  • $ \text{Reactions (1) and (4) are exothermic: the equilibrium constant decreases as temperature increases.} $
  • $ \text{Reaction (3) is endothermic: the equilibrium constant increases as the temperature rises.} $

The pressure-dependent behavior of $K_x$ for reaction (1) vs. reactions (3) and (4) highlights the importance of molar volume changes in gas-phase equilibrium.

Comparison of Reaction Extents¶

An analysis of the reaction extents for the three key reactions shows that reactions (1) and (4) behave similarly. In both cases, the extent of reaction increases with higher pressure and lower temperature. Since these reactions are exothermic, this temperature dependence is thermodynamically consistent.

For reaction (1), the process results in a reduction of the total number of molecules. According to Le Chatelier’s principle, this explains why the reaction extent increases at elevated pressures. Although reaction (4) follows the same trend, the behavior is not directly attributable to a change in the mole count.

In contrast, reaction (3) displays the opposite temperature trend, with the reaction extent increasing at higher temperatures, as expected for an endothermic process. The pressure dependence for reactions (3) and (4) is likely influenced by the coupling of the reaction network:

  • $ H_2O \text{ is a product of reactions (1), (3), and (4).} $
  • $ \text{MeOH is produced in reaction (1) and consumed in reaction (4).} $

On this basis, the pressure-induced shift towards the reactant side for reaction (3) and the product side for reaction (4) can be rationalized through the interdependence of the reaction network and Le Chatelier’s principle.

In their study, the yield dropped from roughly 29% at 210 °C to 9% at 290 °C. Under identical parameters—represented by the green line in our current model—we predict:

  • $ \text{Yield} = 15\% \text{ at } 290 \text{ °C} $

Despite these numerical differences, both studies exhibit the same characteristic behavior: a nearly linear decline in yield that gradually tapers off at elevated temperatures, particularly under low-pressure conditions.

5. Conclusion¶

This study serves as a preliminary step in designing DME synthesis plants that utilize membrane reactors to achieve superior yields. Key findings include:

  1. Optimal Conditions: Favourable yields are obtained at lower temperatures and higher pressures.
  2. Feed Ratios: While higher ($\text{CO}_2:\text{H}_2)$ ratios increase yield, ratios below 1:10 are rarely used in pilot plants [5] due to:
    • Reduced carbon efficiency.
    • Increased reactor volume and cost.
    • Safety concerns associated with large autoclave reactors.
  3. Separation Challenges: Recovering DME from syngas typically requires energy-intensive absorption columns [4,5].

Future Outlook: Membrane-based technologies offer a promising path for the selective removal of $H_2O$, effectively bypassing thermodynamic equilibrium limits. As illustrated by Van Kampen et al. [4], these reactors are a key technology for the sustainable synthesis of DME from ( $\text{CO}_2 ) and ( H_2 $).
